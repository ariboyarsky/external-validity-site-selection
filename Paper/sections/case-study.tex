\section{Case study: which microcredit experiment was worth running}
\label{sec:case-study}

The microcredit data let us test the design rule against experiments
that have already been run. All seven country experiments were run, so we can
hide some of them, score them as candidates from their lending terms
alone, and then reveal their data to check the prediction. This is the
forward version of the leave-one-out exercise in \citet{boyarsky2026},
and it gives a design rule for future experiments a test it rarely
gets, because the future data already exist. We run two exercises. The
first checks the predicted width of a single candidate. The second
checks the choice between two candidates, which is the selection
problem \eqref{eq:minimax}.

\subsection{Specification}
\label{subsec:case-spec}

The specification is the one fixed in the companion paper's case study,
and the replication code reproduces every number. The outcome is household revenues. The context basis holds nine
coded terms: promotion, the annual interest rate, and the average loan
size as a share of income, each coded on the source pool's rank scale,
plus their squares and pairwise products, with every column scaled by
its source-pool standard deviation. Unit-level covariates are the six
standardized household variables. Estimation uses the rank consistent
estimator with three folds, boosted outcome and density-ratio fits, and
the regularized multinomial site classifier. The restriction ladder is
the companion paper's: a magnitude cap of $M=888$ revenue units, one
standard deviation of the outcome, on the control surface and on the
treatment shift, then effect ordering at $\kappa=1$, then the sign
restrictions on the interest-rate terms of the treatment shift. We
report the final rung unless we say otherwise. Coding is frozen at each
target's full six-site source pool, so that removing a source site
changes only whose data enter the moments. The coding uses the lending
terms of the hidden countries, which a designer knows before any
experiment is run, but none of their outcome data. The held-out
benchmarks are the covariate-adjusted design-based estimates with
clustering at each country's randomization unit.

At design time we run the rank test of \cref{sec:selection}, forecast
the revealed scalars with the feasibility-aware neutral forecast
\eqref{eq:feasible-forecast}, and solve the width map
\eqref{eq:width-map} for the predicted interval. The forecast differs
from zero in two of the Bosnia pools of the first exercise, where the
sign restrictions pin the new coordinate away from zero. Then we reveal
the candidate's experiment, refit the estimator on the augmented pool,
and compare.

Every candidate in both exercises expands the hull. This is not
informative about the candidates. With nine basis terms, the affine
hull of four or five profiles has dimension at most four, and a further
profile lies outside it unless it satisfies special linear relations. So
in this application the rank test always passes, and the content of the
design rule is the predicted width.

\subsection{Predicting the width of one candidate}
\label{subsec:case-bosnia}

For each target country, Bosnia and Morocco as in the companion paper,
the first exercise runs over the six five-site source pools. The
missing sixth country is the only candidate for that pool, so this
exercise checks predicted widths. It does not involve a choice. Every
row of \cref{tab:case} ends at the same six-site pool, so the realized
width is the same within each target.

\paragraph{Bosnia.}
Every five-site source pool gives the same result
(\cref{tab:case,fig:case}). The centered profiles of five source sites
span four directions against nine basis terms, the interval at the final
rung is wide and unsigned, with widths between $3{,}525$ and $7{,}291$
revenue units, and no five-site pool supports a conclusion about
Bosnia. The predicted intervals collapse, with predicted reductions of
93 to 100 percent, and the realized six-site interval is
$[37.1,\,127.3]$, width $90$, reductions of 97 to 99 percent. An analyst
holding any five of these experiments, the lending terms of the sixth,
and no outcome data from it would have been told that the sixth
experiment removes almost all of the width that the restrictions leave,
and the realized experiments confirm this.

The forecast cannot determine the location of the collapsed interval,
and the rows differ in location. For four of the six pools the neutral
forecast is an interior point of what the pool allows, the predicted
intervals have widths of $121$ to $286$, and they sit just below and
above zero. The revealed scalars then place the realized interval
strictly above zero, which is the companion paper's signed conclusion
for Bosnia. For these four pools the prediction is wider than the
realized interval. For the other two pools, the ones missing India and
Morocco, the pool's sign restrictions pin the forecast coordinate to a
boundary. The predicted intervals degenerate to near points,
$[129.1,\,138.6]$ and $\{67.5\}$. Here the prediction is narrower than
the realized interval, so the neutral forecast does not always give a
conservative prediction. It is correct in one respect. Once that
direction is resolved, the restrictions themselves nearly determine the
answer, and they already place it above zero. The realized interval
agrees in location and adds back the width, $90$, that the boundary
forecast removed.

\paragraph{Morocco.}
Morocco is the target the companion paper cannot sign, and the design
rule explains why in advance. Even the full six-site source pool leaves
most of Morocco's loading outside the span of the source profiles, so no
single candidate can deliver a collapse. The predicted widths after the
addition lie between $6{,}417$ and $6{,}487$, and the realized six-site
width is $6{,}416$. The predicted gains run from 5.2 to 37.9 percent and
the realized gains from 5.2 to 38.6 percent, matching row by row to
within about one percentage point. Because every row ends at the same
six-site pool, these gains differ across rows mainly because the base
widths differ. They show that the predictions are accurate. They do not
show that the rule chooses well, which is the subject of the next
exercise. Every predicted and realized interval for Morocco stays wide
and unsigned. Strengthening the Morocco conclusion requires a site
outside the observed hull in Morocco's direction, not a sixth source
site from this pool.

\subsection{Choosing between two candidates}
\label{subsec:case-choice}

The second exercise poses the selection problem directly. For each
target and each pair of the target's six source countries, we hide both
countries. The source pool is the remaining four countries, and the two
hidden countries are competing candidates. This gives fifteen choices
per target. At design time we compute the predicted width after adding
each candidate from the four-site pool alone, and the rule picks the
candidate with the smaller predicted width. We then add each candidate's
actual experiment, refit the estimator on the resulting five-site pool,
and record which candidate gives the smaller realized width.

The design-time pick equals the realized pick in all fifteen choices for
Bosnia and all fifteen for Morocco (\cref{fig:case-choice}). These are
not easy choices. The realized widths of the two candidates differ by a
median of 9 percent of the base width for Bosnia and 14 percent for
Morocco, and by as little as 1 percent in the closest pairs, Mexico
against Mongolia for Bosnia and India against the Philippines for
Morocco. The predicted differences track the realized differences, with
a correlation of $0.97$ for Bosnia and $0.999$ for Morocco across the
fifteen pairs. The predicted width of a single candidate is within 7
percent of its realized width for Bosnia, with a median error of 3
percent, and within 1.4 percent for Morocco. For Bosnia the prediction
is too wide in half of the cases and too narrow in the other half. With
four source sites no single addition collapses the Bosnia interval, and
the predicted and realized gains both range from 3 to 68 percent.

At the earlier rungs of the ladder the rule also picks correctly in
every choice for Morocco. For Bosnia it picks correctly in all fifteen
choices under the cap alone and in fourteen of fifteen after effect
ordering is added. The one miss is Mongolia against the Philippines,
where the two predicted widths differ by less than $3$ revenue units out
of about $5{,}100$, so the two candidates are effectively tied.

\subsection{What the exercise does and does not show}
\label{subsec:case-caveats}

Four caveats limit the claim. First, the exercise conditions on the
companion paper's specification: the coded basis, the frozen coding,
and the restriction ladder. The predictions are exact statements about
that specification, not about microcredit at large. Second, the
predictions use estimated base-pool inputs, so they inherit sampling
error. The close match partly reflects that the base and augmented fits
share most of their data. Third, the exercise also tests
\cref{thm:monotone}. The realized additions never widened an interval,
which is consistent with the maintained model but is not implied by it
in finite samples. Fourth, the exercise cannot check the maintained
model at the candidates. Every candidate here expands the hull, so by
\cref{prop:undetectable} a constant conflict at a candidate would be
absorbed into the newly identified coordinate and could not be detected
from the source data, and the compatibility screen of
\cref{sec:monotonicity} has no power. The augmented programs remain
feasible, which is necessary but not sufficient for compatibility. When a screen does fail, as it does on these data for tighter caps
in the supplementary appendix of the companion paper, the right object
to report is the disagreement itself.
