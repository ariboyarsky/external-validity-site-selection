\section{When adding a site can hurt}
\label{sec:monotonicity}

One might hope that new evidence can only help, so that the design
problem is only about how much a candidate helps. Under the maintained
model this is true.

\begin{theorem}[Monotone improvement under the model]
\label{thm:monotone}
Suppose \cref{ass:model} holds for the pool and for candidate $k$, the
augmented eigenvalue condition holds, and the true coefficient vector
satisfies $\beta\in\restrset$. Then $\feasset'\subseteq\feasset$, both
sets contain $\beta$, and for every target $\ct$,
\begin{equation}
  \width(\ct;\pool\cup\{k\})\;\leq\;\width(\ct;\pool),
  \label{eq:monotone}
\end{equation}
with equality for every target when $\cand{k}\in\affhull(\pool)$. In
particular the augmented program is feasible and its interval still
contains the true target effect $\psi_T$.
\end{theorem}

\cref{thm:monotone} is a statement about population bounds. Estimated
widths can still increase after an addition because of sampling error,
even when the model holds.

The assumptions of \cref{thm:monotone} show where it can fail. Every
assumption is a modeling commitment about the new site. The new site
must share the response surfaces $g_a$ and the fixed effects $\beta_a$
of \eqref{eq:response-model}. A site that does not share them, e.g.,
because its implementation or its measurement differs, moves the pooled
normal equations. The normal equations then average conflicting
site-level means, and the identified slice of coefficient space
translates to a compromise that fits no site exactly. The restriction set then cuts the
translated slice at a different cross-section, and the width can move
either way.

\paragraph{A two-site example.}
Take $p=2$ and two source sites with profiles $c_1=(0,0)$ and
$c_2=(1,1)$, the configuration \citet{boyarsky2026} use to illustrate
partial identification. To keep the arithmetic simple, suppose there are
no unit covariates and the sites have equal weight, so the normal
equations for the shift are a regression of the site treatment effects
on the profiles. Let the effect at site 2 exceed the effect at site 1 by
$2$. The pool then identifies $\delta_1+\delta_2=2$ and leaves the
direction $(1,-1)$ free. Impose the box $\shift\in[0,2]\times[-1,1]$ and
take a target that loads on $\delta_1$ alone. The feasible segment is
$\{(\delta_1,2-\delta_1):\delta_1\in[1,2]\}$ and the width is $1$.

Now add a third site with the same profile as site 2, $c_3=(1,1)$, whose
treatment effect equals that of site 1. This site conflicts with the
pool. With equal weights on the three sites, the regression fit at the
profile $(1,1)$ is the average of the effects at sites 2 and 3, so the
identified value of $\delta_1+\delta_2$ moves to the compromise $1$. The
new feasible segment is $\{(\delta_1,1-\delta_1):\delta_1\in[0,2]\}$ and
the width is $2$. A rank-preserving addition doubled the width without
changing the restriction set. Panel (A) of \cref{fig:monotonicity} draws
this example. The size and direction of the change depend on where the
conflicting site sits. If instead $c_3=(2,2)$ with the same effect as
site 1, the regression slope is $0$, the new segment is
$\{(\delta_1,-\delta_1):\delta_1\in[0,1]\}$, and the width stays $1$.

\paragraph{What conflict corrupts first.}
Width is not the right measure of the damage. Consider first a
conflicting site inside the hull. The translated slice can
just as easily cut a narrower cross-section of the restriction set, in
which case the conflicted pool reports a tighter interval around a wrong
location. The precise statement concerns whether the interval covers the true
effect. The translation moves the point the pool identifies, so the
targets that suffer first are the point-identified ones. Their intervals
have width zero, so any movement removes the true effect from the
interval. A wide interval can still cover the true effect after a small
conflict. A point prediction cannot. In the simulation of
\cref{sec:simulation} the conflicted addition changes the worst-case
width by only a few percent in either direction, while the population
interval stops covering the true effect at the span-identified targets
in every replicate. An analyst who sees a surprisingly tight interval after pooling an
interior site should treat it as a sign of conflict rather than as an
improvement.

A conflicting site outside the hull behaves differently. Its conflict
cannot be detected from the source data at all, because the conflicted
data are exactly what some other coefficient vector would produce under
the model.

\begin{proposition}[Conflict at a hull-expanding site is not detectable]
\label{prop:undetectable}
Suppose \cref{ass:model} holds for the pool with coefficients
$\beta^\circ$, and let $\cand{k}\notin\affhull(\pool)$ with
$h_k=\norm{\Pi^\perp(\cand{k}-c_1)}_2>0$ and $\newdir{k}$ of unit
length. Suppose the candidate's arm means are shifted by constants,
\begin{equation}
  \E[Y(a)\mid X,C=\cand{k}]
  =g_a(X)+{\cand{k}}^\top\beta_a^\circ+\Delta_a,
  \qquad a\in\{0,1\}.
  \label{eq:site-shift}
\end{equation}
Then the augmented pool satisfies \eqref{eq:response-model} exactly,
with
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde\beta_a=\beta_a^\circ+\frac{\Delta_a}{h_k}\,\newdir{k},
  \qquad
  \widetilde g_a(X)=g_a(X)-\frac{\Delta_a}{h_k}\,c_1^\top\newdir{k}.
  \label{eq:shift-absorbed}
\end{equation}
Consequently the in-span coordinates $\Bspan^\top\beta_a$ identified by
the augmented pool equal those of the pool, the augmented feasible set
is $\feasset\cap\{\newdir{k}^\top\beta_a=\reveal{a}{k}+\Delta_a/h_k\}$,
and at every target in $\affhull(\pool\cup\{k\})$ the reported effect
differs from the true effect by
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta_1-\Delta_0}{h_k}\,\newdir{k}^\top(\ct-c_1).
  \label{eq:shift-bias}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\cref{prop:undetectable} has three consequences. First, no check that
uses only the source data, including the compatibility screen below, can
detect a constant conflict at a hull-expanding site, because the
conflicted law is the law of a model-consistent world with coefficients
$\widetilde\beta$. The value of a hull-expanding site therefore rests on
an assumption that its own data cannot check. Second, whenever the augmented program is feasible, the width still
weakly shrinks, since the augmented feasible set is again $\feasset$ cut
by two hyperplanes. The damage is in location. It falls on the targets
that load on the new direction, which are the targets the candidate was
chosen to help, and targets inside $\affhull(\pool)$ are not affected.
Third, the bias \eqref{eq:shift-bias} is inversely proportional to the
candidate's distance $h_k$ from the hull. Distance does not affect
the identified set under the model, but a farther candidate is less
exposed to a site-level conflict of a given size. This gives a reason to
prefer a distant candidate among candidates that resolve the same
direction. For an interior candidate the situation is reversed. No
affine function of the profile can equal one at the candidate and zero
at every pool site, so a constant conflict there cannot be absorbed into
the coefficients, and the data carry information about it.

\paragraph{Screens.}
Two checks separate refining additions from corrupting ones.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Rank screen, before the experiment.} Test
  $\cand{k}\notin\affhull(\pool)$. A candidate that passes adds the
  equality constraints \eqref{eq:refinement} and, under the model,
  cannot widen anything (\cref{thm:monotone}). A candidate that fails
  changes no identified quantity, so any movement of the estimated set
  after adding it, beyond sampling noise, is itself evidence of
  conflict.
\item \emph{Compatibility screen, after the experiment.} For an
  interior candidate the data overidentify the in-span coordinates. The
  projections $\Bspan^\top\beta_a$ are estimated by the pool and, once
  the experiment runs, by the candidate site jointly with the pool.
  Compare the pool's minimum-norm fit with the augmented fit on the
  in-span coordinates. The two estimates share the pool's data, so the
  comparison should use the variance of their difference, computed from
  the joint influence functions of the two fits, as in a Hausman test. A
  large discrepancy indicates the conflict mechanism above. The check is
  an overidentification test of the kind used to detect an invalid
  instrument. For a hull-expanding candidate the screen has no power
  against a constant conflict (\cref{prop:undetectable}), and a
  discrepancy can arise only from conflicts that vary with $X$. The
  forecast version of this screen, before the experiment, compares the
  candidate's predicted outcomes under the pool's fit with whatever
  prior information exists about outcomes there. For a hull-expanding
  candidate this prior information is the only available check.
\end{itemize}
When a screen fails, the analyst should report both analyses, the bounds
with and without the new site, together with the discrepancy between
them. Dropping the new site assumes that the pool is right. Pooling
assumes that the model is right. The screens make the disagreement
visible so the analyst can decide which assumption to keep.
